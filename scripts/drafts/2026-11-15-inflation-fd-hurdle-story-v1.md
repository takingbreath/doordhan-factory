## English story for approval

SBI's posted one-year FD card rate is 6.25%. October's inflation reading was 6.09%. That gap looks small.

Now take a one-year example: prices rise 6.09%, and 30% of your interest goes in tax.

To hold your purchasing power steady, you need 8.7% before tax.

Why? You keep 70% of the interest; 8.7% becomes 6.09%.

So an 8.7% return can still mean zero real gain. Part of the interest being taxed is only making up for higher prices.

Your personal hurdle is higher than the inflation headline.

## Progression and factual boundaries

Apparently close FD/CPI numbers → hypothetical one-year period and tax assumption → surprising gross return needed → show where the number comes from → even that return merely preserves buying power → tax can apply to the inflation-compensation portion of interest.

The actual October CPI figure measures the preceding twelve months; it does not forecast the next twelve. The example explicitly assumes the same price growth over its own one-year horizon. Do not remove that condition while tightening or describe this as every current depositor's measured loss.

Thirty percent is an assumed effective tax on the example's interest, not a statement that every saver pays it or that a statutory 30% slab includes all levies. Actual treatment can differ. The 8.7% is a one-year gross return, not necessarily an advertised nominal card rate with a different compounding convention. SBI's rate is a dated posted card rate; the calculation does not purport to reconstruct a specific SBI account's net maturity value.

No recommendation to chase an 8.7% product, move/break an FD, change asset classes or buy/sell a security. The lesson concerns the measurement of purchasing power. No food-index detour, RBI forecast or tax-law advice is needed for this story.
