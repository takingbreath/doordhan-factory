# Starlink — pricing power without market leadership

## English script proposal

Musk doesn't have to beat Jio to put pressure on its profits.

He's said Starlink must be affordable in India, though no tariff is out.

If it becomes a credible alternative for home internet, Jio and Airtel may have to offer better deals—or find it harder to raise prices.

Here's the catch: they could keep a customer and still earn less from that connection.

Add the cost of improving their networks, and future profits could grow more slowly.

Starlink isn't another nationwide mobile network yet. But for Jio's IPO, the question goes beyond how many customers it could lose: how much pricing power could it lose?

## Business chain: hypotheses, not forecasts

1. Entry conditions: commercially competitive total cost (terminal + installation + service), capacity, reliability and regulatory approvals. Affordable intent alone establishes none of these.
2. Direct effect: win some reachable household/enterprise customers, serve underserved customers, or act as backup. Underserved growth may expand the market rather than take Jio customers.
3. Second order: where genuinely substitutable, increased choice may reduce incumbents' ability to raise prices; discounts/bundles could retain a customer at lower revenue or contribution. Incumbents could instead keep prices and improve quality; outcome isn't certain.
4. Third order: slower ARPU growth plus extra network/retention spending could lower cash-flow growth or returns on invested capital. If investors revise those expectations, the valuation they are willing to pay may change. Not a prediction the IPO or listed stock must fall.
5. Counterweight: Jio/Airtel distribution agreements may allow them to benefit from selling Starlink; own satellite ventures are potential hedges, subject to execution and approvals. Competition is not necessarily a pure zero-sum price war.
