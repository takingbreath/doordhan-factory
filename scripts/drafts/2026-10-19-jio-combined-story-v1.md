## Full English story

Musk is publicly fighting Ambani just as Jio prepares to go public. For an investor, that raises a useful question: how much of Jio's future can Starlink actually threaten?

Start with a number from a different competitor: Airtel.

In the June quarter, Jio reported monthly ARPU of ₹215.6. Airtel's India mobile ARPU was ₹264. A gap of roughly ₹48.

At Jio's current customer base, an extra ₹48 in monthly ARPU would add roughly ₹31,000 crore in annual revenue. That's about the size of the entire IPO.

It is an illustration, not a forecast. Their customer mixes differ, and Jio's reported ARPU covers more than mobile. We cannot assume Jio can simply charge every customer another ₹48. The calculation shows how much even a small change in monetisation matters at its scale.

And getting that revenue into a spreadsheet is easier than collecting it.

Jio has invested heavily in its network, while unlimited 5G remains bundled into qualifying plans. More usage can help through upgrades, retention and network efficiency. It doesn't automatically produce a separate 5G premium.

Likewise, announcing a tariff hike and realising the full increase in ARPU are different things. Some customers upgrade. Others downgrade, delay a recharge or leave. What matters is the revenue Jio actually retains after those choices.

Jio is already highly profitable. The IPO question is how much more earnings and cash flow it can generate from the network and customer base it has built.

Which brings us to its other major growth lever: home broadband.

Jio's fixed-broadband customer base grew about 55% in FY26. Yet only about 21% of Indian households had a fixed-broadband connection. AirFiber helps Jio reach homes where running fibre is difficult or expensive.

That is where Starlink becomes relevant to this story.

Its current dish-based service is expensive compared with Indian broadband, and the capacity in each coverage area is shared. It cannot simply replace Jio's mobile network at the same economics. Satellite-to-phone is a separate, developing opportunity; it doesn't establish an Indian mass-market mobile rival today.

But some of the homes that are difficult for Jio to reach are also the homes where satellite connectivity makes sense.

If Starlink becomes cheaper and adds sufficient capacity, Jio could encounter more competition while that home-broadband market is still opening up. It might have to offer more value, accept lower revenue per connection or spend more to win those homes. None of those outcomes requires Starlink to replace hundreds of millions of Jio SIMs.

That is the conditional risk Jio describes in its own filing: if satellite solutions become more cost-effective or scale faster, its broadband offerings could become less competitive and require additional investment.

Jio has an immediate monetisation challenge: earn more from the scale it already has. It also has a growth opportunity: connect homes it hasn't reached yet. Starlink's present overlap is much more direct in the second business than the first.

For investors, the questions are how much of a tariff hike Jio can retain, and how profitably it can capture the next wave of broadband customers. The Twitter fight cannot answer either.

Jio has demonstrated it can win customers. The IPO asks how much more it can earn from them—and what it will cost to win the next ones.
