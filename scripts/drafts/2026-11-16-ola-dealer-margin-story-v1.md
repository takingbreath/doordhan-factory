## English story for approval

Ola is closing most of its own stores and moving to dealers.

That adds a dealer's cut. Ola expects gross margin to dip.

But running its own stores also costs money, even when scooters don't sell. As those stores close, Ola expects operating costs to fall.

And dealers pay when they order, then hold the stock themselves. Ola can get cash before the final buyer arrives.

That's why it expects the vehicle business's operating margin to improve.

The bet is to give up some margin per scooter, while reducing the cost—and the cash—needed to run the business.

## Curiosity progression and limits

Why could a margin decline accompany improvement? → closing company stores introduces a dealer cut → store operations also cost money → reveal the upfront payment and inventory shift → distinguish the operating-margin forecast from gross margin → land the cash/cost trade-off.

The company says it stopped D2C-store orders on 1 November and most stores would close by December. Do not imply every outlet is already shut or that Ola has stopped selling scooters. Its first dealer stores opened on 4 September; do not present the transition as a new decision made on 16 November.

“Dealer's cut” and the comparison with running one's own stores are our plain-language economic interpretation of the rollout. The source forecasts a slight gross-margin decline in Q3/Q4 and improved Auto operating margin; it does not disclose a universal dealer commission or prove that all costs disappear. Keep “expects,” “can” and “the bet.” Do not turn a forecast into achieved profitability or free cash flow for the consolidated company.

Dealer payment and stockholding can improve working capital before end-customer purchases. This is not a finding of fake demand or improper revenue recognition. The company also reports improving sell-through, repeat orders and about 15 days of channel stock, and says supply is its constraint. This proposal makes no adverse conclusion about those claims. Orders, dealer dispatches, registrations and customer demand must not be treated as interchangeable if adding figures later.

The story does not need a share-price move, quarterly loss-to-revenue comparison, predicted recovery or buy/sell instruction. In particular, do not call accounting losses cash burn or assume channel change alone fixes service quality, production or demand.
