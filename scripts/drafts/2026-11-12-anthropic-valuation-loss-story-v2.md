## English story for approval

Anthropic reported a $104 billion net loss for the first nine months of 2026. That isn't a $104 billion cash-burn figure.

It raised some early money through financing that can turn into shares. Those financing claims are recorded as liabilities at their estimated value.

As Anthropic's valuation rose, those claims were valued more highly too. The increase became an expense, without an equivalent cash payment.

That fair-value expense was $94.3 billion—most of the nine-month loss.

But this doesn't make the business profitable. It still recorded a $1.26 billion operating loss over the same nine months.

The loss headline wasn't just measuring how the business performed. It was also reflecting how valuable the financing claims had become.

## Story progression and limits

Large loss → cash-burn assumption challenged → financing claims → higher valuation increases their accounting value → non-cash expense explains most of the headline → operating-loss check → distinguish business performance from financing revaluation.

The valuation effect is the filing's explanation, not an invented numerical example. The expense line covers convertible notes, derivative liabilities and SAFEs; the narrative simplifies the relevant financing mechanism without claiming every startup uses identical accounting. The residual after subtracting this line is not cash burn, operating loss or a clean adjusted profit. No subtraction-based cash-burn claim is used.

Non-cash does not mean the claims are economically irrelevant or dilution-free. No prediction of profitability, reversal of every charge, post-IPO earnings or listing gains is made. A smaller GAAP operating loss is also not a measure of cash spent.
